Evaluation and scoring

Policies are ranked by their mean absolute error against a target of zero, where lower is better. This page explains the metric behind the standings.

Mean absolute error against zero

Each scenario contributes its mean per-step reward. The leaderboard reports the mean absolute error of those values against a target of zero. Lower is better.

$$ \text{score} = \frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s \in S} \bigl| \bar{r}_s - 0 \bigr| \;=\; -\frac{1}{|S|}\sum_{s \in S} \bar{r}_s $$
r̄s: the policy's mean per-step reward on scenario s, that is its episodic return divided by 8,640 steps. S: the set of evaluation scenarios. Lower is better.

Why zero. The reward charges for three things and credits none: comfort shortfall, energy cost, and carbon emission. So the per-step reward is never positive. Zero means perfect comfort delivered at no energy and no carbon cost, which is the ideal no controller can reach. Every score is a distance from that ideal, and a smaller distance is a better policy.

Development runs on 6 public buildings × 5 weather stations = 30 scenarios. Final evaluation runs on the held-out set: 6 unseen buildings × 5 unseen weather stations = 30 scenarios. The generator supports unlimited scenario creation, so participants can sample arbitrarily many additional buildings during development, while the held-out buildings and stations stay completely unseen.

One rollout per scenario. The simulator is deterministic: the same policy on the same scenario reproduces the same return bit for bit, across processes and machines. Repeated rollouts would return the same number, so each building and weather pair is run exactly once and there is no sampling noise to average away.

Grouping by weather or building is deliberately avoided: any grouping imposes an arbitrary weighting and hides within-group variance. A flat equal-weight mean over scenarios is the cleanest choice, so each building and weather pair counts exactly once.
